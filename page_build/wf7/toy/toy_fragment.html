Toy worlds: which part of BCA can help

exploratory Five notebooks in the repository, run on toy seeds only: four experiments and one notebook that gathers them. Nothing here is ThrusterChain evidence or changes a pre-registered rule. They ask which part of BCA (Bayesian Conformal Aggregation; Methods, part 7) can change a decision for the better.

The bottom line

What the four experiments found, in brief. Each point is explained step by step below, and every number comes from toy worlds, not from ThrusterChain.

  • As extra caution in training, BCA did not help. The learners on this page use BCA's band as a width: a margin of error on the critic's values (the critic is the learned estimate of each action's value) that makes training more cautious where the margin is large. In experiments 1 and 2 the width was calibrated where it was measured, yet it improved no decision and cost return: in experiment 2 the two width rules lost 0.425 and 0.375 per episode against the best possible choices, against 0.122 for simply refusing actions with too little data.
  • As a gate on a risky action, BCA did help. A gate reads the risky action's held-out tries, its recorded outcomes that training never saw, and either lets the action through or falls back to the safe action. In experiment 2 every gate that read these tries closed 38% to 87% of the room, the return that refusing thin data loses against the best possible choices; fed shuffled tries, none kept its gain.
  • BCA's tail gate keeps a safety promise for the data in hand. It lets the action through only if BCA is 95% sure that at least 90% of its outcomes are at least as good as the safe action's. In experiment 2, in any world where more than 10% of the action's outcomes fall below the safe action's (an unsafe world), it let the action through in at most 3% of seeds (repeat runs), against up to 38% for split conformal prediction, the standard rule. But with equal weights BCA is a textbook test: it made the same decision as the exact binomial test in 99.6% of cases, and it cannot say yes with fewer than 29 tries.
  • BCA's mean gate earns the most at equal safety (experiment 3). The mean gate asks instead whether the action beats the safe one on average. With every gate tuned to accept at most 5% of the harmful proposals in every world, BCA's mean gate earned 0.084 per situation, about twice the next gate (0.040, another gate built on BCA) and 4.5 times the best gate not built on BCA (0.019). With no safety requirement, the plain held-out average earns more (0.158 against 0.045).
  • BCA's weights keep its promises when deployment meets conditions in a different mix than the recording (experiment 4). Ignoring this shift, the tail gate let unsafe actions through in up to 89.9% of seeds; with weights, in at most 4.3%. The price is evidence: under a strong shift it needs about 83 tries before it can say yes at all, against 29 with no shift.
  • The recommended role: a certificate on each deviation, not a penalty in training. A deviation is a risky action that the policy, the rule that picks the actions, wants instead of the safe one. Compare its held-out outcomes with the safe action's value; use the mean gate for return at a fixed safety level, the tail gate for "rarely worse than the safe action", and weights only when the conditions differ (What it all adds up to).

Why toy worlds

On ThrusterChain one answer costs hours of training, and many things change at once. In a toy world every true value is known exactly, a whole experiment runs in seconds to a few minutes, and each notebook isolates one mechanism. The price is realism: a toy shows what can work or fail in principle, never what works on ThrusterChain.

Kill rules. Experiments 2 to 4 were pre-registered. Each claim has a kill rule: a result, written down before the run, that would shelve the claim (set it aside as unsupported). A claim that survives its kill rule is alive. Each notebook prints a fingerprint of its kill rules, a SHA-256 hash that changes if a single character changes, and by each notebook's own record that fingerprint was taken before its first run. The world: two steps, two contexts

Experiments 1 and 2 share this world; experiments 3 and 4 use smaller worlds of their own, explained in their parts. It is built in six steps.

  1. An episode is two decisions.

    The first decision is the first stage, the second the last stage. Before the first, a context, 0 or 1, is drawn; the controller sees it, and it stays fixed for the episode. The steps do not change each other: the first choice does not change what the second one pays.

  2. Two actions at each stage.

    The controller picks safe, which pays a fixed amount, or venture, which usually pays more on average but can be noisy or harmful. An episode's return is the sum of its two rewards.

  3. A recording, and a critic fitted on it.

    The data are recorded episodes of a recording controller that ventures with chance 0.5 at the first stage, 0.25 at the last stage in context 0, and never at the last stage in context 1. Some recorded episodes train the critic, a table of averages (one per stage, context and action) fitted on them and then frozen; the others are held out, never used in training, so that BCA can check the critic on rows it has not seen.

  4. The gap.

    The cell the recording never visits, venture at the last stage in context 1, is the gap cell, or gap. It plays ThrusterChain's removed full-throttle command: the critic can only guess its value. What the gap truly pays differs between the experiments' worlds, versions of the toy that differ only there, so the recording cannot tell them apart.

    Worked: the average of venture at the gap in the harmful world average=0.8 × (−5) + 0.2 × 2 = −4 + 0.4 = −3.6

    So: in the harmful world venturing at the gap loses 4.6 against safe's 1 on average; in the harmless world it pays 2, a gain of 1. The recorded data are the same in both worlds.

  5. A known shift.

    Context 1 is 15% of the recorded episodes but 65% at test (also called deployment): the episodes the controller is judged on. Nothing else changes. So the exact weight of a recorded row, its context's share at test divided by its share in the recording, is known.

    Worked: the exact weights of a recorded row w0=35 divided by 85 = 7 divided by 17 = 0.412 w1=65 divided by 15 = 13 divided by 3 = 4.333 Check: over the recording's mix, 85% and 15%, the weights average 1: 0.85 × 0.412 + 0.15 × 4.333=0.35 + 0.65 = 1 Over the test mix, 35% and 65%, they average w̄ (read “w-bar”): w̄=0.35 × 0.412 + 0.65 × 4.333 = 0.144 + 2.817 = 2.961

    So: a recorded row from context 1 counts 4.333 times, and one from context 0 only 0.412 times, because the test meets context 1 relatively more often than the recording does. Weighted BCA also multiplies the extra weight it gives an unseen next row by w̄ = 2.961 (see BCA's tools, below).

    Worked: what weighting costs in evidence Experiment 1's 300 held-out episodes (seed 7) hold 264 rows of context 0 and 36 of context 1. Their effective number, how many equally weighted rows they are worth, is (sum of weights)² ÷ (sum of squared weights): neff=(264 × 0.412 + 36 × 4.333)² divided by 264 × 0.412² + 36 × 4.333² = 264.71² divided by 720.76 = 97.2

    So: under this shift the 300 held-out episodes count as about 97 equally weighted ones: the 36 rows from context 1 carry 59% of the weight.

  6. Exact scoring.

    Because every reward law is known, each rule's expected return is computed exactly, not sampled. Regret is the expected return lost against the best possible choices, per episode at the test mix of contexts; 0 is perfect.

BCA's tools, in toy terms

BCA turns a critic's misses on held-out rows into a threshold: a number it is 95% sure that at least 90% of future misses stay at or below. Each held-out row gets a score, how far the critic missed (bigger is worse). BCA gives every sorted score a random weight, plus one extra weight for an unseen next score; in each such random draw it finds the score where the running total of weight reaches 90% of all the weight, the draw's crossing. The random draws are BCA's posterior: Bayesian language for its spread of plausible answers. Its threshold is the 950th smallest of 1,000 crossings, or the plain 90th percentile of the scores if that is larger. Split conformal prediction, the standard rule, takes one high percentile of the held-out scores, just above the 90th to allow for the number of rows; its promise holds on average over many data sets, not for the one data set in hand. The experiments use BCA in four ways:

  • A width. The threshold times a scale gives a margin of error on the critic's value. Subtracting it from the value is extra caution. The learners on this page use the width in a related way, inside training: as a dose that strengthens the pull toward the recorded actions where the width is large (TD3+BC with BCA, Methods, part 7). Experiments 1 and 2 test it.
  • A tail gate. A gate reads held-out tries of one risky action (its recorded outcomes that training never saw) and either lets the action through or falls back to the safe action, the fallback. BCA's tail gate lets it through only if BCA is 95% sure that at least 90% of the action's outcomes are at least as good as the fallback: its tail promise. With equal weights this is a textbook test, the exact binomial test: with n tries, b of them below the fallback, it says yes when b is at most x(n), the largest count x such that, in a world where exactly 10% of outcomes fall below the fallback, n tries would show x or fewer below it with chance at most 5%. Experiments 2 to 4 test it.
  • A mean gate. The same random weights, asked about the average: in each of 10,000 draws take the weighted average gain of the tries, with the extra weight placed on the floor, the worst gain a try can have; say yes if at most 5% of these averages are below 0. Its mean promise: it says yes to an action that is worse than the fallback on average at most 5% of the time. Experiments 3 and 4 test it.
  • Weights. Each row's random weight is multiplied by its exact weight, and the extra weight by w̄, so that BCA answers for the test's mix instead of the recording's. Experiments 1 and 4 test it.
Worked: why a tail gate needs at least 29 tries A draw never crosses when the extra weight alone holds more than 10% of all the weight. With equal weights that happens with chance 0.9n, and BCA needs it in at most 5% of draws: 0.928=0.0523, more than 0.05: refuse, whatever the 28 tries show 0.929=0.0471, at most 0.05: a yes is possible With 30 tries, how many may fall below the fallback? Add the binomial chances of 0, 1, … tries below, for a world where 10% of outcomes fall below: chance of 0 below=0.930 = 0.0424, at most 0.05 chance of 1 or fewer=0.0424 + 30 × 0.1 × 0.929 = 0.0424 + 0.1413 = 0.1837, more than 0.05 ⇒x(30) = 0: with 30 tries the tail gate says yes only if none falls below the fallback

So: the tail gate cannot say yes with fewer than 29 tries, and needs many more before it tolerates even one bad outcome (46 tries allow one).

Worked: the mean gate when every try gains the same If all n tries gain the same g, the share of draws whose weighted average falls below 0 has an exact form, (−floor ÷ (g − floor))n. With 29 tries and a floor of −1: g = 0.11=(1 divided by 1.11)29 = 0.0485, at most 0.05: yes g = 0.10=(1 divided by 1.10)29 = 0.0630, more than 0.05: no

So: the floor stands in for the disaster the tries might be hiding, so a small, steady gain must be large enough to outweigh it.

Experiment 1: does BCA's width make the policy choose better?

exploratory demo bca_discrete_toy.ipynb: a demonstration on seed 7, whose numbers had been seen before, plus a 200-seed sweep computed exactly. There is no kill rule: its numbers describe, they decide nothing.

Each method sets a band around the critic's value from 300 held-out calibration episodes, to hold the one-step target (the reward plus the critic's value of what follows) 90% of the time; coverage is the share of test targets inside. Widths are a band multiplier, from split conformal or BCA, unweighted or weighted by the exact weights, times a count scale: 1 over the square root of the cell's training rows. Each method takes the recorded action with the higher value minus width. Support only, the baseline, also keeps to recorded actions but subtracts no width; scale only subtracts the count scale with a multiplier of 1; no penalty trusts the critic everywhere, including its placeholder of 2.5 for the gap, an optimistic guess that no data support.

Experiment 1 in four parts

The idea
BCA's calibrated width, subtracted from the critic's values as extra caution, makes the policy choose better.
What we tested (and the kill rule)
Seven rules on seed 7, in two worlds that differ only at the gap (harmful and harmless), then the same on 200 more seeds; coverage and return computed exactly. A demonstration: no kill rule.
What happened
Weighting kept the width's coverage under the shift, but no width improved a choice. Every calibrated width turned away venture at the last stage in context 0, a good but noisy action, for 0.175 more regret than support only in each world. Over 200 seeds BCA earned less than support only in 182 (mean −0.162 per episode), weighted BCA in 144 (−0.129).
Why
Every width is one multiplier per stage times the count scale, so calibration sets the penalty's strength, not its place. The last stage's multiplier comes from venture's own noisy payouts (0 or 3), so the width mostly measures reward noise, and it says nothing about the gap, where the decision matters. Refusing the unseen action at the gap is done by support alone.
  • Weighting kept 90% coverage under the shift. At the first stage, under the test mix, unweighted bands held 69% (split conformal) and 78% (BCA) of targets, reaching 90% in 1 and 9 of the 200 seeds; weighted, 99.3% and 99.9%, in 191 and 199. (At the last stage no method can fall below 91.25% under the test mix.)
  • Refusing the unseen action did the safety work. Every method but the critic's plain choice refuses the gap, whatever the calibration says; in the harmful world the plain choice earns 2.99 less per episode than support only.
  • BCA's width set the penalty's strength, not its place. Its widths are the count scale times one multiplier per stage, so BCA ranks actions as the count scale does, only more strongly. The extra strength fell on a recorded, better action with merely noisy rewards (0 or 3, against a certain 1): BCA earned less than support only in 182 of the 200 seeds (mean −0.162 per episode), weighted BCA in 144 (−0.129).

Worked on seed 7. Why only 67.5% coverage at the first stage? There, only one cell has an uncertain payout: venture in context 1, which test episodes visit with chance 0.65 × 0.5. BCA's first-stage multiplier is 0, because only 16 of the 300 held-out scores there are above 0, under 10%; so both of that cell's payouts fall outside. With weights it rises to 6.2714, the larger of that cell's two scores (4.34 and 6.27), so both payouts fall inside.

Worked: coverage at the first stage, seed 7 Coverage = 1 − (chance that a target comes from that cell) × (share of its payouts outside the band): BCA, test mix=1 − (0.65 × 0.5) × 1 = 1 − 0.325 = 0.675 BCA, recording's mix=1 − (0.15 × 0.5) × 1 = 1 − 0.075 = 0.925 weighted BCA, test mix=1 − (0.65 × 0.5) × 0 = 1 Where the weighted multiplier comes from. The 16 scores above 0 are all context-1 rows; the other 20 context-1 rows and all 264 context-0 rows score 0. Running total of weight, out of 264.7: scores of 0=264 × 0.412 + 20 × 4.333 = 108.7 + 86.7 = 195.4, which is 73.8%: short of 90% adding the 12 scores of 4.34=195.4 + 12 × 4.333 = 195.4 + 52.0 = 247.4, which is 93.5% So the plain weighted 90% point is 4.34. BCA's random draws, with the extra weight w̄ = 2.961 for an unseen row, put the 95% point of their crossings one score higher, at 6.27.

So: the recording sees context 1 only 15% of the time, too rarely to lift the 90% point above 0; the test sees it 65% of the time. Weights count each context-1 row 4.333 times and restore coverage.

And why does every calibrated width turn venture away at the last stage in context 0? The critic values safe at 1.000 (396 training rows, count scale 0.05025) and venture at 1.626 (107 rows, scale 0.09667). BCA's last-stage multiplier is 16.8212, which is exactly the score of venture's own 0 payout:

Worked: the choice at the last stage, context 0, seed 7 safe=1.000 − 16.8212 × 0.05025 = 1.000 − 0.845 = 0.155 venture=1.626 − 16.8212 × 0.09667 = 1.626 − 1.626 = 0.000 Venture wins only while the multiplier stays below the break-even value: break-even=1.626 − 1.000 divided by 0.09667 − 0.05025 = 0.626 divided by 0.04642 = 13.49 Every calibrated multiplier is above it: 16.82 unweighted, 14.21 weighted. The cost, in each world: regret=0.35 × (1.5 − 1) = 0.175

So: the width pulls venture down to its worst payout, 0, while safe, certain to pay 1, still has 0.845 subtracted. Scale only, with a multiplier of 1, keeps venture and has no regret there.

Experiment 2: where can BCA change a decision?

exploratory bca_channel_toy.ipynb: kill rules written down and fingerprinted (bb9e0325…dbc3) before the first run, by the notebook's own record; the repository cannot confirm the order, because the notebook was first committed on 15 Oct, after its runs. 200 toy seeds.

This notebook adds 5 to 100 held-out tries of venture at the gap, the survivors (named after ThrusterChain's surviving full-throttle steps), which the critic never trains on. The critic guesses about 1.5 there, against 1.0 for safe. Ten worlds differ only in the truth at the gap, one for each advantage (how much venture beats safe on average there): +1.0, +0.5, +0.25, +0.1, −0.1, −0.25, −0.5, −1.0, −2.0 and −4.6. Each comes in two shapes: shortfall (every outcome a little worse) and tail (a rare catastrophe of −5). Rewards carry noise (standard deviation 0.2). Every world and shape uses the same recording and the same random numbers behind the survivors.

Worked: the tail shape at advantage −0.25 Venture pays −5 with chance p = (1 − advantage) ÷ 7, and 2 otherwise: p=1 − (−0.25) divided by 7 = 1.25 divided by 7 = 0.179 average=0.179 × (−5) + 0.821 × 2 = 2 − 7 × 0.179 = 0.75 = 1 + (−0.25)

So: both shapes have the same average at each advantage; here it is 0.75, below safe's 1, so falling back is right. In the shortfall shape 89.4% of outcomes fall below 1.0, in the tail shape 17.9%.

Regret is the expected return lost against the best choices, per episode at the test mix; 0 is perfect. The room is the regret of the best rule that never reads the survivors, refusing choices with fewer than 20 training rows: 0.1215. A rule's gain is the share of the room it closes. A world is unsafe if more than 10% of venture's outcomes at the gap fall below safe's 1.0, and a rule's unsafe-deviation rate is how often it ventures at the gap in unsafe worlds.

Worked: the room Falling back at the gap loses the advantage whenever it is positive (+1, +0.5, +0.25 and +0.1 of the ten), in context 1, which the test meets 65% of the time: at the gap=0.65 × 1 + 0.5 + 0.25 + 0.1 divided by 10 = 0.65 × 0.185 = 0.12025 elsewhere=0.00130 (in 2 of the 200 seeds the critic's noise flipped a first-stage choice) room=0.12025 + 0.00130 = 0.12155, printed as 0.1215

So: the room is what the oracle, a rule that knows the world, wins back. A gain of 1 means the oracle's choices, 0 means no better than refusing thin data, below 0 means worse. A gate decides only the gap, so it can close at most 0.12025 ÷ 0.12155 = 98.9% of the room.

The rules. BCA's width as built is subtracted from the critic's value at every action, or multiplies a pull toward the recorded actions, as in the learners BCA is added to, such as TD3+BC (Methods). Gates read the survivors and decide only the gap: venture there, or fall back to safe. The step-1 gate copies the restructured BCA's first step on ThrusterChain (below): fall back if BCA is 95% sure that the survivors' over-predictions (the critic's value minus the outcome) exceed the other last-stage rows' by over 0.10. The harm test falls back if BCA is 95% sure that over 10% of outcomes fall below the critic's value of safe; the tail certificate ventures only if BCA is 95% sure that 90% beat it. Experiment 3: can a BCA gate raise return? The mean gate

exploratory bca_mean_gate_toy.ipynb: kill rules written down and fingerprinted (50a0f49d…b1d3) before the first run, by the notebook's own record; 200 toy seeds of 20 situations, seeds 0–99 used only to tune settings and seeds 100–199 for every result.

Experiment 2's tail gate asks whether 90% of outcomes beat the fallback, the wrong question for return. This experiment tests the mean gate, which asks about the average, in a world of its own built in four steps:

  1. Situations and candidates.

    In each situation the policy can take a safe action, worth 0, or one of several candidate actions. Each candidate has a true average gain over safe: above 0 it is good, below 0 harmful. Only the scoring knows these numbers.

  2. Two sets of tries.

    The recording holds 5 to 100 tries of each candidate, plus a separate held-out set of the same size.

  3. The critic proposes, the gate decides.

    Looking only at the first set, the critic proposes the candidate with the best average, if that average is above 0. With many candidates it is often fooled by one that got lucky. Looking only at the proposal's held-out tries, a gate accepts it or stays safe. The score is the true average gain of the final choice; staying safe scores 0.

  4. Three shapes, 36 worlds.

    A try's gain is steady (the true average plus noise of spread 1), a rare disaster (a small gain of 0.2 to 0.6, but sometimes a loss of 8) or a rare jackpot (a small loss of 0.1 to 0.4, but sometimes a gain of 8). Gains stay inside a known range: −3 to 3, −8 to 3 and −3 to 8; the bottom of the range is the floor. A world combines one shape, one share of good candidates (10%, 30% or 50%) and one number of candidates per situation (1, 3, 10 or 30): 36 worlds.

The rivals: the plain average of the held-out tries (yes if above 0); a shrunk average, pulled toward the average of all candidates in the seed's 20 situations; BCA's cautious average, (sum of tries + floor) ÷ (tries + 1), which counts the worst possible gain as one extra try; the classical 95% lower bounds on an average (the t-test, which assumes roughly bell-shaped outcomes; Hoeffding's bound, from the range alone; and empirical Bernstein's, from the range and the spread; the last two promise 5% for any outcomes inside the range); a count rule (yes with at least 30 held-out tries); BCA's tail gate and split conformal's; and the mean gate deciding all 20 situations together under a shared 10% harm budget (the Benjamini–Hochberg method). A gate's harm rate in a world is the share of harmful proposals it accepts; a world's allowance on a 5% rate is 5% plus three standard errors for its number of harmful proposals.

Worked: why rare disasters fool the t-test The notebook's own example, outside this experiment's worlds (whose disasters lose 8): a candidate gains 0.4 on 95% of tries and loses 10 on the rest. With 29 tries: true average=0.95 × 0.4 + 0.05 × (−10) = 0.38 − 0.5 = −0.12: harmful chance of no loss=0.9529 = 0.23 In those clean samples every try shows 0.4 and the spread is 0, so the t-test's bound is 0.4 and it says yes. Told that the floor is −10, the mean gate puts this share of its weighted averages below 0: clean sample=(10 divided by 10.4)29 = 0.32, far more than 0.05: no

So: the t-test accepts this harmful action in about 23% of samples instead of 5%; the mean gate refuses every sample, because the extra weight on the floor stands in for the disaster a clean sample may be hiding.

With equal weights BCA is a textbook test (experiment 2); its one part that is not textbook is its weights. This experiment asks whether they keep BCA's promises when the held-out tries come from a different mix of conditions than deployment, in a world of its own:

  1. One deviation, five conditions.

    A policy wants to deviate, take a risky action instead of a well-recorded fallback, everywhere in a region of states, or fall back everywhere. The region has five conditions, from mild (0) to harsh (4).

  2. A gain, usually small, sometimes a disaster.

    A try's gain over the fallback is usually about 0.5 (0.5 plus noise of spread 0.2, kept between −1 and 2), but sometimes a disaster of −4. The deployment's overall disaster chance, the rung, is one of 0, 0.02, 0.05, 0.08, 0.10, 0.12, 0.15 and 0.20; how it spreads over the conditions is the profile: everywhere alike, mostly in harsh conditions, or mostly in mild ones.

  3. A shift.

    The recorded tries came mostly from mild conditions; deployment may meet harsh ones more often. A try's exact weight is its condition's share at deployment divided by its share among the recorded tries.

    A world combines a profile (3), a rung (8), a shift (none, moderate or strong) and a number of held-out tries (30, 60, 120 or 240). A world is unsafe when more than 10% of outcomes at deployment fall below the fallback, and harmful when the average gain at deployment is below 0. A gate's deviation rate in a world is the share of seeds in which it deviates; in an unsafe or harmful world, that is how often it breaks its promise. Worked: which rungs are unsafe or harmful A usual gain falls below 0 with chance 0.0062 (0.5 is 2.5 spreads above 0). At deployment: rung 0.10, share below=0.10 + 0.90 × 0.0062 = 0.1056, above 0.10: unsafe rung 0.10, average gain=0.90 × 0.5 + 0.10 × (−4) = 0.45 − 0.40 = 0.05: still good rung 0.12, average gain=0.88 × 0.5 + 0.12 × (−4) = 0.44 − 0.48 = −0.04: harmful

Worked: the weights under the strong shift w0 … w4=5 divided by 35, 10 divided by 25, 15 divided by 20, 30 divided by 12, 40 divided by 8 = 0.143, 0.400, 0.750, 2.5, 5 w̄=0.05² ÷ 0.35 + 0.10² ÷ 0.25 + 0.15² ÷ 0.20 + 0.30² ÷ 0.12 + 0.40² ÷ 0.08 =0.0071 + 0.0400 + 0.1125 + 0.7500 + 2.0000 = 2.9096 The average squared weight over the recording's mix is also w̄, so n recorded tries are worth about n ÷ w̄ equally weighted ones: effective tries, 120 recorded=120 ÷ 2.9096 = 41.2

The gates: BCA's tail gate and mean gate, each unweighted (answering for the recording's mix) and weighted (BCA's weights, the extra weight at w̄; the weighted tail gate is the one built in calibration/wbcp.py, with 1,000 draws); weighted split conformal (Tibshirani and others, 2019), whose promise holds only on average over data sets; two simple valid ways to handle a shift, both of which throw evidence away: rejection sampling (keep each try with chance weight ÷ largest weight, then run the exact test) and a stratified exact bound per condition; a weighted t-test; and plain and weighted averages. No proof covers the weighted mean gate.

What it all adds up to

The recommended role for BCA: a certificate on each deviation, not a penalty in training. The notebook's seven recommendations, from toy evidence only:

  1. Use BCA as a gate on deviations, not as a width in training. A width calibrated at recorded actions cannot see the actions the recording lacks, and its caution costs return (experiments 1 and 2). The toys cannot judge a dose in training, because in them a critic's errors never spread through its own training targets; what they show is that neither way of using a width beat simply refusing thin data. So the width doses in the learners that host BCA, the multiplier on the pull toward the recorded actions in TD3+BC and similar learners and the penalty multiplier in conservative Q-learning, are the wrong place for claims about BCA.
  2. Measure a deviation against the fallback's value, not against other rows plus a margin: deviate only if a bound on the deviation's held-out outcomes beats the fallback's value, which the critic estimates on data. In experiment 2 this cut regret from 0.051 to 0.028 and drops the step-1 gate's hand-picked 0.10. A harm test still ventured in 27.6% of cases in unsafe worlds; a safety promise needs a gate that asks for proof of safety.
  3. Match the gate to the promise you need. For the most return at a fixed safety level, BCA's mean gate (0.0845 against 0.0400 and 0.019 in experiment 3, and by a narrow margin in experiment 4, both with settings tuned on worlds whose truth is known). For "rarely worse than the fallback", BCA's tail gate (at most 3% of seeds in any unsafe world in experiment 2, against up to 38% for split conformal). With no safety level required, the plain held-out average (experiment 2's regret 0.016; experiment 3's gain 0.158).
  4. Use weights when the held-out tries come from a different mix of conditions than deployment. They kept both promises in experiment 4, where ignoring the shift broke them. For the weighted mean gate, prefer the version with the largest weight on the worst case.
  5. Compute equal-weight BCA exactly. With equal weights the tail gate is the exact binomial test. With 1,000 random draws only 4 of 8 score sets matched the exact answer at 28 and at 29 scores, and exact arithmetic lowers the evidence floor from 36 rows, the project code's allowance for sampling error, to 29. Keep the random draws for weighted sets of scores.
  6. Describe it plainly. With equal weights, BCA's tail gate is a textbook tolerance limit (Wilks'), and its mean gate is Gaffke's bound computed with BCA's random draws. The defensible claims are a safety promise for each decision that holds for the data in hand, and the most return at a fixed safety level; the claim that BCA's Bayesian posterior adds information over a textbook test is not supported.
  7. Budget the evidence. The tail gate needs at least 29 held-out outcomes per decision with no shift, about 39 under the moderate shift and about 83 under the strong one.

What this does not show

  • Toy seeds only. Nothing here is ThrusterChain evidence or evidence about the learners that host BCA, and nothing is confirmatory.
  • The deviation was given. Every gate was handed the deviation and read real, independent held-out tries of it. A real gate must find its deviations and group similar ones, and each split of the evidence costs tries.
  • Floors and weights were known exactly. A wrong floor voids the mean gate's promise, and estimated weights are untested.
  • Each decision stood alone. None of the worlds lets a critic's errors spread through its own training targets, the main failure in offline learning.
  • Two guarantees are open. The mean gate's guarantee rests on an unreviewed 2026 preprint, and BCA's own weighted mean gate, with the average weight on the worst case, has no proof.

Where the toys do not carry over

  • A quiet comparison group. ThrusterChain's comparison group was quiet (its 90% point stayed between −0.049 and −0.031 in step 1's run record), so the toy's step-1 weakness, a noisy comparison group, did not arise.
  • One-step targets. ThrusterChain's scores use one-step targets, so the critic's value of the next state would stay in a harm test. The toy's last stage has no next state, so there the critic drops out.
  • Thin evidence. A tail gate needs 29 rows. In step 1, no-stall seed 3 had 29, and BCA's random draws gave it no finite caution size (947 of BCA's 1,000 random draws crossed; 950 were needed; step 1's run record). In the toys, computed exactly as they recommend, 29 equally weighted rows are just enough for a finite answer. Under a shift they need more: experiment 4's tail gate needs about 39 held-out outcomes per decision under its moderate shift and about 83 under its strong one. If step 1's 29 to 60 full-throttle steps per seed and world had to be weighted for a shift like those, they would be enough for some seeds and worlds under the moderate shift and for none under the strong one.
  • The shape of the harm. The stall is shortfall-shaped (past 80% every command pushes less, rather than failing rarely), the shape in which the toy's plain average served return best: with 30 survivors it ventured in 99% to 100% of seeds where venturing pays and in at most 1% where it loses.
  • A given region. In the toys the risky action was given and its held-out tries were real outcomes. Step 1 was given the full-throttle region, and its outcomes are one-step scores from critics.

Result F: what the toys predict, as a hypothesis

exploratory hypothesis Predictions from toy mechanisms, not ThrusterChain results. The control that would test them has not been run.

Result F is the weighting test's one case where BCA's band, used in training, clearly raised return. The band was weighted at the actor's own commands, and the command part of the weight was given the recording controller's exact command likelihood. That likelihood is zero wherever the controller never gives a command; on expert data, that includes every command past 80%. Where it is zero, the band refuses the command, and result F's rule for refused commands gives it no value credit: in the actor's training it earns no more value than the recorded command at that row. (The page calls this rule the fixed fallback; it is not this section's fallback action.)

On the strong test, against the band penalty as first planned (version 5's unweighted lower edge), return rose by 2.0 points on medium-expert and 5.8 on expert, every seed positive. An untargeted control, the same amount of no value credit at randomly chosen rows, gained about 1 unit of return, against 25.0 and 74.2 units for the rule. Only the actor was trained, against a frozen critic, and the rule was designed after a first version had lost 9.8 points on medium-expert. Two things are open: the control without the band, the same no-value-credit rule alone with no band, was not run; and with estimated weights the gain held only on expert (2.3 points).

  • Most of the gain would come from the no-value-credit rule, not from the band's calibration. In experiment 1, refusing the never-tried action did all the safety work, by construction for every method; BCA's calibrated width only added caution on a recorded action with noisy rewards, and cost return (−0.162 per episode). On ThrusterChain, the weighting test's band penalty as first planned (its version 5), on its own, gained nothing (90.6 against 90.7 points on expert). So the toys predict that the untested control would recover most of result F's gain.
  • Past 80%, a gate could not do better than the rule. The expert recording holds no commands past 80%, so there are no held-out tries there, and every valid toy gate falls back without evidence (the tail gate cannot say yes below 29 tries). There, a gate and the no-value-credit rule would decide alike.
